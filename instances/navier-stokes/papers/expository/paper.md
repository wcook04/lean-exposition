# Guide to the mathematical representations

A definition specifies an object or condition. A theorem asserts that its conclusion follows from its hypotheses.

| Object | Representation and comparison with the paper |
|---|---|
| **Velocity and pressure** | The velocity is an actual function u:ℝ×ℝ³→ℝ³; pressure is a real scalar function on the same domain. Smoothness, solenoidality and the equations are separate requirements. A pressure-gradient representative in a certificate is instead a supplied vector field annihilating solenoidal tests; its name alone does not identify a classical pressure. See §1.1 and §§7.1–7.3. |
| **Space and torus** | Space is three-dimensional Euclidean space with Lebesgue measure. The torus is represented by functions on ℝ³ with period one in each coordinate. The formal global torus conclusion restricts the velocity's periodicity and allows any smooth pressure. The paper's §1.1 torus convention makes both velocity and pressure periodic. |
| **Smoothness and physical time** | Global solutions are smooth up to the boundary of [0,∞)×ℝ³, take the prescribed datum at zero, and satisfy the equations at t>0. Their total functions also have values at negative times, on which these hypotheses impose no conditions. In particular no two-sided time derivative or momentum equation at t=0 is asserted. Compact support and decay of the datum are separate conditions. |
| **Weak fields and normalized time** | Certificate fields use θ=s/S and period one. Period integrals use [0,1]×ℝ³; endpoints do not change Lebesgue integrals. Supplied G,H,g represent ∇v,∇²v and S⁻¹∂θv+½v+½y·∇v only when the distributional identities are imposed. The perturbation class requires v(θ)∈L⁶ almost everywhere and G,tr H,g∈L², without requiring v∈L². This represents (7.4)–(7.5); the paper also chooses a continuous time representative. |
| **Norms and indices** | Vectors use Euclidean norm. Matrix energy sums use Frobenius norm; pointwise norms of derivative maps use operator norm. Their dimensional equivalence does not preserve explicit constants. Derivative matrices store vector component first, derivative direction second, transposing the paper's displayed convention. The paper's Fourier label (ι₁,ι₂) corresponds to raw time-angle frequencies (2ι₂−ι₁,ϰι₁). See (B.2), (B.12), (B.15). |
| **Self-similar profile** | W,q solve (1.2) and recur by W(s+S,y)=R W(s,R⁻¹y), with S>0 and R orthogonal, possibly orientation reversing. Pressure recurrence is not separately required. The smooth-profile condition displays Hessian and pressure-gradient decay in addition to (1.3). Remark 1.2 derives the Hessian decay from the paper's profile assumptions; the pressure-gradient clause is separately identified in the card. The weak formulation uses measurable representatives and explicit distributional derivatives. |
| **Certificate and numerical data** | Data supply functions, finite arrays and proposed constants. Validity additionally requires the quantified identities and inequalities of §7.3 and Appendix B. Supplying arrays does not verify them. Unit-normalized row fields eₐ with weights τₐ represent the paper's scaled fields √τₐeₐ. At the cell level a stored test weight tₐ becomes the certificate weight tₐ/c. Earlier box/ball estimates and the steady fractional extension are distinguished from the extended classical certificate. |
| **Breakdown conclusions** | For every ν>0 the statements give some smooth solenoidal datum excluding a global smooth unforced solution: compactly supported on ℝ³, with a uniform-in-time energy bound imposed on candidate solutions; spatially periodic for the torus. The datum may depend on ν. These conclusions omit the finite terminal time, solution before that time, two-sided rate and profile approximation in Theorem 1.1. The cell-system theorems assume their complete certificate conditions. |

**Conventions that matter.** Natural-number exponents and cutoffs are nonnegative integers, and subtraction between them stops at zero. Division by zero and the real square root of a negative input are zero. A derivative where differentiability fails, a nonintegrable ordinary integral, and a nonsummable real infinite sum also receive zero. Nonnegative integrals may equal infinity; conversion of infinity to a real number gives zero. An unbounded real supremum is assigned zero. Applicable cards state the hypotheses needed for the ordinary analytic interpretation; these defaults are not estimates.
